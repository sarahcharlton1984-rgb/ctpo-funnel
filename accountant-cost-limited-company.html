How much should an accountant cost for a limited company?

The honest answer is that the number matters less than what sits behind it. Two firms quoting the same fee can be selling completely different things.

Sarah Charlton, tax strategist and founder of Tax 4 Pros

Right. Every director asks this question and almost nobody gets a straight answer, because the profession has spent decades quoting a headline figure and then billing around it.

So here is how accountancy pricing actually works, what should be inside the fee, and the specific things to ask before you agree to anything.

Why the range is so wide

You will see monthly fees for a limited company quoted anywhere from under a hundred pounds to several hundred. That range is real, and it is not simply a matter of some firms being greedy. It reflects three genuine differences.

What is actually included

The cheapest quotes usually cover the bare statutory minimum: your annual accounts, your corporation tax return, and possibly one self assessment. Everything else is billed as it arises. Payroll, VAT, a second director's return, the confirmation statement, and very often the questions.

Whether anyone does any thinking

This is the big one. Filing your accounts is a process. Looking at whether your setup still makes sense is a judgement. Plenty of firms only sell the first and let you assume you are buying both.

How much of it is you

A low fee often assumes your bookkeeping arrives clean and finished. If you are handing over a shoebox, someone is doing that work, and it will appear on an invoice at some point.

The test that cuts through all of it: ask what is not included. A firm with a genuinely fixed fee can answer that in one sentence. A firm that cannot is telling you something.

The extras that turn a quote into a bill

When you are comparing firms, these are the line items that most often sit outside a headline monthly fee. Ask about each one by name.

  • A second director or shareholder's self assessment. Common in husband and wife companies and routinely charged separately.
  • Anything at Companies House beyond the confirmation statement. Share issues, changes of officer, registered office.
  • Catching up bookkeeping. If your records are behind, someone has to fix them before the accounts can be prepared.
  • Dealing with HMRC. Enquiries, penalty appeals, payment plans. Some firms sell insurance for this rather than including it.
  • Ad hoc advice. The phrase to listen for is "that would be chargeable." It is the moment the relationship changes.
  • Year end tax planning. Often presented as a premium service. It is the part that makes the rest worth paying for.

Cheap is not the same as good value, and expensive is not either

A firm charging a hundred pounds a month and filing everything correctly is not doing a bad job. If your company is straightforward, you take a small salary, and there is genuinely nothing to plan, that may be exactly right for you and you should not pay more.

The question is whether your situation is actually that simple. Once you are taking meaningful profit out of a limited company, the difference between a considered pay structure and a default one is usually worth several times the entire annual fee. At that point paying less for a firm that never looks at it is a false economy, and it is one that repeats every single year.

The way to judge it: not what the fee is, but what it is a percentage of. If a review of how you pay yourself saves you more than the year's fees, the fee was never the relevant number.
