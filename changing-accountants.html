How to change accountants, and why it is easier than you think

Most directors who want to move do not, because they imagine an awkward conversation and a mountain of admin. Neither happens.

Sarah Charlton, tax strategist and founder of Tax 4 Pros

Right. The single most common thing I hear from directors who have outgrown their accountant is not that they are happy. It is that moving sounds like a hassle.

So they stay another year. Then another. And the thing they were unhappy about carries on quietly costing them.

Here is exactly what happens when you switch, in order, so you can judge it properly rather than imagining it.

The five steps, start to finish

  1. You have a short conversation with the new firmFifteen minutes is usually enough for both sides to know whether it is a fit. A firm worth moving to will tell you if you are already set up sensibly and do not need them.
  2. You sign two documentsAn engagement letter setting out what they will do and what it costs, and a 64-8 authorising them to deal with HMRC on your behalf. Both are done electronically.
  3. The new firm writes to the old oneThis is called professional clearance. It is a standard courtesy letter asking whether there is any professional reason they should not act for you, and requesting your records. Your new accountant sends it. You do not.
  4. Your records transferPrior year accounts, tax computations, the bookkeeping file, payroll records. If you are on cloud software the new firm simply gets added and the old one removed. Nothing is rebuilt from scratch.
  5. You are liveUsually within a week or two of signing, depending on how quickly the old firm responds. Then the actual work starts.

You do not have to tell your old accountant yourself. The clearance letter does it. If you would rather send a short note out of courtesy you can, and one line is plenty, but it is not required and it is not expected.

The five things people worry about

"Do I have to wait until my year end?"

No. You can move at any point in your financial year, and mid year is often better. The whole value of a good accountant is in the decisions taken before the year closes, so arriving with three months left gives them something to work with. Arriving the day after the year end means the first year is pure catch up.

"What if my old accountant refuses to hand things over?"

They can hold documents if you genuinely owe them money, which is called a lien, and it is rare. Settle any outstanding invoice and the issue disappears. In practice almost every handover is routine and the profession deals with them constantly.

"Will it be awkward?"

Far less than you think, because it is not personal to them. Firms lose and gain clients every month. The clearance letter is a form they have processed a hundred times.

"Will I lose my history?"

No. Your filed accounts sit at Companies House, your tax history sits with HMRC, and both are accessible to your new firm once the 64-8 is in place. The transfer is a convenience, not a dependency.

"Is there a gap where nobody is acting for me?"

No. The authorisations overlap. You are covered throughout.

The better question: should you move at all?

Being annoyed about a fee is not on its own a reason to switch. A cheaper firm doing the same narrow job leaves you exactly where you are. Here are the reasons that actually justify it.

  • Nobody has ever asked how much you need to take out of the business. If the salary and dividend split has been the same for years and nobody has revisited it, nobody is doing the thinking.
  • You only hear from them at the deadline. Tax planning happens before the year end. A firm that surfaces in month eleven has already missed the window.
  • You have stopped asking questions because they get billed. This is the most expensive dynamic in the profession and it is completely invisible on the invoice.
  • The business has changed and the setup has not. You took on staff, bought a property, started a second company, or your profit doubled. The structure that suited a one person startup rarely suits that.
  • Something big is coming. A sale, a restructure, a move abroad, an inheritance tax problem. These need planning years ahead, not at the point they happen.

If none of those apply, stay where you are. Genuinely. Plenty of directors are well served and do not need to move.

If several of them apply, the cost of another year of not moving is almost always larger than anything you would save by staying, and unlike a fee it repeats silently every year.
