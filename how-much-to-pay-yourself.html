How much should I pay myself as a company director?

There is a question that comes before the salary and dividend split, and almost nobody gets asked it.

Sarah Charlton, tax strategist and founder of Tax 4 Pros

Right. Here is the question.

How much do you actually need to take out of the business this year?

Not how much profit did you make. Not what did you take last year. Not what does the software suggest. How much do you need.

In years of doing this I have found that almost no director has ever been asked it. The default is to extract whatever is in there, in whatever way seems normal, and find out in January what it cost. And that default is expensive, because the tax on money you take out and the tax on money you leave in are completely different numbers.

Work it out in this order

  1. What do you need to live onYour actual household outgoings plus a sensible margin. This is a real number and most people have never written it down. Start here, because everything else is a decision layered on top of it.
  2. What is coming that needs cashA house move, school fees, a car, a tax bill on last year. Known commitments in the next twelve to eighteen months belong in the number, not in a surprise later.
  3. What does the business need to keepWorking capital, a VAT and corporation tax buffer, anything you are saving toward. Money extracted and then lent back to the company is the most expensive mistake in this whole area, and it happens constantly.
  4. What is left overThis is the part where the real decision lives. Not how to extract it, but whether to extract it at all this year.

Steps one to three give you a number. Step four is where the money is, and you cannot get to it without the first three.

The mechanical answer, for the money you do need

For profit you genuinely need in your hands, the standard structure for most directors is a salary at the personal allowance of £12,570, then dividends on top.

The salary sits inside your personal allowance so there is no income tax on it, there is no employee National Insurance at that level, it is still deductible for the company, and it keeps your National Insurance record going for state pension purposes. The company pays employer National Insurance on the part above £5,000, roughly £1,136, which is usually outweighed by the corporation tax saved.

Dividends then carry no National Insurance at all and are taxed at 10.75, 35.75 or 39.35 percent depending on your band, after a £500 allowance.

The traps at particular numbers

£50,270

Cross this and dividends above it jump from 10.75 to 35.75 percent. If you are taking slightly over, and you did not need the last part, that last slice was expensive.

£60,000 to £80,000, if you have children

The High Income Child Benefit Charge claws back Child Benefit across this range. For a family with two or three children the effective rate on income in this band is considerably higher than the headline, and it catches people who have no idea it exists.

£100,000

The personal allowance tapers away at £1 for every £2 above it, which creates an effective rate of around 60 percent between £100,000 and £125,140. This is the single most punishing band in the UK system and it is entirely avoidable if somebody plans for it before the year end.

Every one of these is a timing problem, not a rate problem. Each is manageable if it is seen coming and almost none of them can be fixed afterwards.

For the money you do not need

This is the part that separates a good year from an expensive one. If step four left you with profit you do not need this year, taking it anyway is a choice with a price attached. The alternatives are usually worth more than any adjustment to the split.

  • A company pension contribution. Deductible for the company, no National Insurance, and it moves money into your name without an extraction charge. Often the single biggest lever a profitable company has.
  • Leaving it in the company. Tax you have not triggered is the cheapest tax available. Retained profit stays available for next year, for investment, or for a different structure later.
  • Spreading across tax years. Dividends are taxed when declared. Two smaller dividends either side of 5 April can cost meaningfully less than one large one.
  • Family shareholdings. Where genuinely commercial and properly structured, more than one set of allowances and bands changes the arithmetic. It has real conditions and it is not a paper exercise.

All of these depend on your circumstances and every one of them has a condition or a catch. Anyone presenting them without those is not doing you a favour.

The honest summary

If you take one thing from this: the split is a second order question. The first order question is how much you need, and once you have answered that properly the rest tends to fall out of it.

If nobody has sat you down and asked you the first question, that is worth knowing, because it usually means nobody is looking at the second one either.
