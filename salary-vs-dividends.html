Salary vs dividends in 2026/27: how the split actually works

The low salary plus dividends route still wins for most directors. Here is the mechanism, the rates behind it, and the point where the advantage runs out.

Sarah Charlton, tax strategist and founder of Tax 4 Pros

Right. This is the most asked question in owner managed business and it is also the one most often answered with a rule of thumb somebody heard in 2019.

The split matters, but not for the reason most people think. It is not that dividends are magically cheaper. It is that salary and dividends are taxed by completely different systems, and the two systems overlap badly at low levels and barely at all at high ones.

The two systems

Salary is a cost to the company, so it reduces the profit that corporation tax is charged on. But it carries National Insurance twice, once from the company and once from you, and then income tax on top at 20, 40 or 45 percent.

Dividends are paid out of profit after corporation tax has already been charged. So the company pays tax first, and then you pay dividend tax on what comes out. But there is no National Insurance on a dividend at all, from either side, and the dividend rates are lower than the income tax rates.

The whole thing turns on National Insurance. Dividends escape it entirely. That is the advantage. Everything else is detail.

Corporation tax between £50,000 and £250,000 of profit runs through marginal relief, which produces an effective rate above 25 percent in that band. That is a detail worth knowing, because it means the cost of a dividend is not flat across profit levels.

Why £12,570 of salary

The standard structure is a salary at the personal allowance, then dividends on top. It is standard for three reasons that stack.

  • No income tax on the salary. It sits entirely inside the personal allowance.
  • No employee National Insurance. At that level you are below the point it starts.
  • The salary is still deductible for the company, so it reduces corporation tax even though almost nothing is paid on it personally.

The company does pay employer National Insurance on the part above £5,000, which is roughly £1,136 on a £12,570 salary. That is a real cost and it is worth knowing about. It is usually still outweighed by the corporation tax saved, and the salary also keeps your National Insurance record ticking for state pension purposes, which a dividend does not.

Where the advantage runs out

This is the part the rule of thumb misses. The dividend advantage is largest at modest profit levels and shrinks as profits rise, for two reasons.

First, corporation tax gets more expensive. Once profits pass £50,000 you are into marginal relief and then the main rate, so the money funding your dividend has been taxed harder before it reaches you.

Second, the dividend rates themselves climb, and at higher levels the gap between the dividend rate and the income tax rate narrows considerably.

Put those together and at high extraction the two routes converge. Extracting everything from a very profitable company costs roughly the same whichever label you put on it.

Which is the actual insight: once the split stops mattering much, the question is no longer how you extract. It is whether you should be extracting it all in the first place.

The levers that beat the split

By the time the salary and dividend question is close to neutral, these are usually worth considerably more, and none of them appear in a simple two route comparison.

  • Pension contributions. A company contribution is a deductible expense and carries no National Insurance. Frequently the single largest lever available to a profitable company.
  • Retaining profit rather than extracting it. Tax you have not triggered is the cheapest tax there is. If you do not need the money this year, taking it anyway is a choice with a price.
  • Family shareholdings. Where genuinely commercial and properly structured, using more than one person's allowances and bands changes the arithmetic materially.
  • Timing across tax years. Dividends are taxed when declared, which gives you a lever most people never use.

Each of these depends entirely on your circumstances, and each has conditions attached. None of them is a trick, and anybody presenting them as free money is skipping the conditions.

The question to start from

Not "what is the optimal split." Start with how much you actually need to take out of the business this year. Not what the profit was, not what you took last year. What you need.

Once that number exists, the rest of it orders itself. Before it exists, you are optimising a decision nobody has actually made.
